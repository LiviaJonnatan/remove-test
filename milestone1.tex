\section{Background and Motivation}

Before a federal agency changes a regulation, it has to take public comments and consider them,
and fake comments have flooded that process before.
The comments on a rule are collected in a \emph{docket}, and on big rules most of them are
\emph{form letters}. An advocacy group, the \emph{sponsor}, writes one letter and asks its supporters
to send it, and all the copies together make up a \emph{campaign}.

This is the norm when real people send the letter. It is fraud when the names are stolen, and on
arrival the two look the same. The FCC's 2017 net neutrality repeal drew 22 million comments, and
four years later New York's Attorney General found nearly 18 million of them were fake [4].

Agencies post one representative letter per campaign with a count of copies, a practice the
Administrative Conference of the United States endorsed in 2021 [5]. On our development docket, EPA's 2019
``waters of the United States'' rule, that meant 507{,}230 comments under 201 campaign records,
92 with no known sponsor.

Near-duplicate detection groups close copies [1, 6], but its recall falls as wording changes, and
the 1.3 million likely fake FCC comments data scientist Jeff Kao [2] found were all reworded from one template. Nothing available tells an
analyst who is behind a campaign or whether its signers look real, so genuine campaigns get written
off alongside fake ones. Our main user is the agency analyst who has to summarize and answer the
comment record, and journalists, attorneys general and advocacy groups benefit too. Kao's analysis
and the NY AG report drew us to the problem.

\paragraph{Problem statement} Given a rulemaking docket, find its comment campaigns and show each
one's size, timing, arguments and evidence of whether real people sent it, without labelling any
individual comment as fake.

  \item \lead{Key attributes} Letter text, a receipt date per comment or filing, and per campaign

        street addresses.
  \item \lead{Relevance} EPA's grouping labels about 116{,}000 letters with their campaign. In the six largest of the 13 full-letter campaigns,
        1{,}678 are \emph{personalized}, with enough of the sender's own words to differ in length from
        the template by over 10\%. MinHash, a fast estimate of word overlap, matches only 7\% of them to their template at a
        threshold of 0.8 and 78\% at 0.5. Untuned \texttt{bge-small}, our starting embedding model [3], puts 99.7\% above cosine 0.8 to their
        template, but different templates score a median 0.85 against each other, so no single threshold separates campaigns. \emph{Reworded} letters, which we will generate with an LLM, are a
        separate stress test.
  \item \lead{Data quality concerns} One API call per record means 11.4 hours per key for the
        development docket, so we use five keys, and ECFS takes about 24{,}000 calls paged by date.
        Four campaign PDFs are scans needing OCR. EPA's counts for the full-letter campaigns exceed the letters
        attached by about 1{,}900. Signer names are hashed with a secret key, never shown.
\end{leads}

\section{Scope and Objectives}

A campaign is the set of comments built on one template, with its sponsor recovered where we
can. EPA grouped by template, so its grouping is our label, and templates with two sponsors are
reported, not forced. The central
challenge is recognizing one campaign across personalized and reworded letters without judging any
individual commenter. We reuse an open embedding model, HDBSCAN, BM25 and a hosted LLM, and build
the rest, as Appendix B shows.

\Needspace*{5\baselineskip}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Ingest} comments, attachments and signer lists into one record per letter.
  \item \textbf{Group} near-exact copies into one representative each with MinHash [6].
        We embed the representatives together with the remaining letters and
        cluster them with HDBSCAN, so a reworded letter lands with its template. For FCC 17-108 we
        cluster a sample stratified by MinHash group size and assign the rest to the nearest one
        under the same similarity threshold the endpoint uses.
  \item \textbf{Fine-tune our own embedding model}, starting from \texttt{bge-small-en-v1.5} [3]. It
        reads 512 tokens, enough for the median 290-token letter and the personal text at its end, and learns with
        a contrastive loss on real letters, with personalized copies as hard positives. It is cross-validated by leaving out one of the eight sponsors behind the 13 full-letter
        campaigns at a time.
  \item \textbf{Characterize} each campaign by size, personalized share, any sponsor named in its
        attachments, timing where comments have their own dates, and signer checks such as ZIPs outside
        the stated state, names on several campaigns, or claimed versus listed counts. Features are scored in median absolute deviations from the docket median and averaged into a reading
        order, not a fraud score.
        Signer checks exist for 21 campaigns and personalized share for 13, so the other 180 rank on size and sponsor status. The baseline ranks by copy count.
  \item \textbf{Link} each campaign to the rule sections it argues about, trying BM25 first, then
        embedding retrieval, and an LLM only if it beats both.
  \item \textbf{Serve} a dashboard and a live endpoint that returns a pasted comment's campaign,
        or none below a threshold set on individual comments.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Success Criteria}

\begin{leads}
  \item \lead{For the analyst} Every campaign and its evidence on one screen.
  \item \lead{Grouping} \emph{B-cubed F1}, averaging precision and recall over letters, on the
        held-out sponsor's personalized letters within a whole-docket clustering, against exact match,
        MinHash at 0.5 and 0.8, and the untuned model.
  \item \lead{Robustness} Held-out sponsors' letters reworded by an unseen LLM at five strengths, 20\% to 100\% of sentences. The fine-tuned model should beat MinHash at each strength.
  \item \lead{Transfer} The same comparison on the Clean Power Plan docket, unseen sponsors
        reported separately, and on Kao's 1.3-million-comment FCC cluster [2]. If fine-tuning does not
        help, we ship the untuned model.
  \item \lead{Ranking} How many of the NY AG's campaigns land in our top 20 on FCC 17-108. The rest are
        audited by hand, not counted as false positives.
  \item \lead{Evidence and links} Hand audit of the 92 unknown-sponsor campaigns, and top-1 accuracy on
        50 hand-mapped ones, chosen so at least half argue a specific section.
\end{leads}

\section{Minimum Components}

\begin{leads}
  \item \lead{Large, heterogeneous data} Half a million EPA comments and 24 million FCC filings
        from three APIs, in text, PDF, spreadsheets and scans.
  \item \lead{Scalability} Sampled clustering for FCC 17-108, and a stateless API on Cloud Run,
        later GKE.
  \item \lead{Complex model} Few, noisy labels from 13 EPA-grouped campaigns, and transfer
        to unseen dockets.
\end{leads}

\paragraph{Learning emphasis} Fine-tuning, RAG, containers and Vertex AI from the course, and MinHash,
HDBSCAN and label-scarce evaluation from outside it.

\paragraph{Application mock} Appendix A, which follows an analyst from a docket to one
campaign's evidence, using real records.

\paragraph{Fun factor} We get to answer a question that took a state attorney general four years and
subpoenas. The first unknown-sponsor campaign we opened
(66{,}777 comments) was a CREDO Action petition claiming 110 more signers than its file lists.

\section{Limitations and Risks}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Making a genuine campaign look fraudulent would harm the group and every real signer, so the
        app ranks campaigns, never people, shows the evidence behind each rank, and labels each sponsor as
        recorded by EPA or found in an attachment.
  \item The model would learn EPA's grouping mistakes, so we check 200 letter pairs and 100
        personalized letters by hand before training.
  \item The NY AG findings cover few campaigns, so FCC results are case studies. If the download runs late, we use those
        campaigns plus a random sample.
\end{itemize}
